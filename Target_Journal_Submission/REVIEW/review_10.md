# Review 10 — Health-services / implementation reviewer

Manuscript: *Detecting Glaucomatous Visual Field Progression From Few Tests Without False Alarms: An Open Benchmark With a True No-Change Arm* (target: Ophthalmology and Therapy)

Numbers below were checked against `results/metrics.json`, `results/statistics.json`, `results/descriptives.json` (including `tests_to_first_detection` and `false_flags_per_1000`), `tables/*.csv` and `results/raw_results.csv.gz`. Calculations that the manuscript does not report are marked **[reviewer calculation]**, with their method stated so the authors can reproduce or reject them.

## Summary

The paper argues that a clinic should not escalate glaucoma treatment on a flag from an unvalidated progression detector. It says that a plain OLS trend of mean sensitivity is a well-calibrated default, and that a "shuffle audit" of a clinic's own records is a cheap first check of any new detector. Converting results into real-world units is the right move ("false flags per 1,000 stable eyes", "fields to first flag", 1.3 years between fields), and few papers in this journal do it (`journal_level_profile.md`: decision/clinical-utility analysis in 2/37 papers).

But the implementation analysis stops one step short of what a service planner needs. The per-1,000 figure covers a single review, while clinics review the same eye at every visit. Positive predictive value at realistic prevalence is not reported. The years-to-detection consequence is left implicit. The shuffle audit is described as something "any clinic can run", but the paper does not specify what the clinic needs, how many eyes, which cut-off, or how often the audit itself gives the wrong answer. Generalisability rests on one Humphrey 24-2 clinic with 1.3-year test intervals, one 30-patient weekly retest series, and a younger Chinese Octopus cohort.

## Issues

### 1. MAJOR — "False flags per 1,000 eyes at one review" understates the real burden; cumulative false flags over repeated reviews are not reported
- **Location:** Methods, Real-world measures ("expected false progression flags per 1,000 stable eyes at one review"); Results ("D1 would raise about 44 false progression flags, D6 about 224 and D8 about 277").
- **Verification:** `descriptives.json` false_flags_per_1000, k = 3: D1 44.3 (28.3–62.7), D6 223.5, D8 277.3. Correct as stated.
- **Problem:** A stable eye in follow-up is re-analysed at field 3, 4, 5 and 6. **[Reviewer calculation]** Using Artes windows that start at the first field, the same time-scale draw index, and "ever flagged at k = 3, 4, 5 or 6", averaged over seeds from `raw_results.csv.gz`: D1 ≈ 15%, D5 ≈ 15%, D4 ≈ 15%, D6 ≈ 39%, D8 ≈ 51%, D9 ≈ 23%, D2 ≈ 7%, D7 ≈ 3%. This is approximate, because the draw index may not tie the same UWHVF date pattern across k. Even so, it suggests that roughly 1 in 7 truly stable eyes would get at least one false trend-test flag within six fields. That matters for clinic workload and patient anxiety even with the "valid" detector. It also re-orders the detectors: the event rule and rate rule accumulate far fewer false flags than D1.
- **Fix:** Add a pre-specified-style "cumulative false-flag rate over sequential reviews" for each detector (per 1,000 stable eyes followed to six fields), with patient-bootstrap CIs. Discuss confirmation rules (e.g. a flag must persist on the next field), which are standard practice and would change both false-flag and delay figures.

### 2. MAJOR — No positive predictive value or flags-per-true-progressor at realistic prevalence
- **Location:** Results, False alarms; Discussion para 5.
- **Problem:** Clinicians act on a flag. The quantity that matters is how many of the flags are real. **[Reviewer calculation]** Using GRAPE's own prevalence (27/263 = 10.3% MD-label progressing) and Table 3, at k = 3 per 1,000 eyes: D1 ≈ 46 true + 42 false flags (PPV ≈ 0.52); D2 ≈ 61 + 190 (PPV ≈ 0.24); D8 ≈ 68 + 348 (PPV ≈ 0.16). Combining UWHVF sensitivity with Artes false-alarm rates at an assumed 20% prevalence (26/132 test eyes with ≥ 8 fields were reference progressors): D1 PPV ≈ 0.52, D6 ≈ 0.39, D8 ≈ 0.19, D2 ≈ 0.79. For D8, about four of every five flags would be false. The last line also shows that the MD-rate rule (D2), not D1, has the better PPV at three fields on point estimates. This cuts against the Abstract's "a simple trend test remains a strong default" and is never discussed.
- **Fix:** Add a short table of flags per 1,000 eyes (true, false, PPV, number of false flags per true progressor detected) at two or three stated prevalences, with the caveat that prevalence in the reference set is artificial (26:26 by design). Discuss D2 honestly: its k = 3 performance looks good, but its sensitivity falls to 23% at k = 4–6.

### 3. MAJOR — Years to detection, the patient-relevant quantity, is left implicit and partly misleading
- **Location:** Results ("D1 flagged 69% by the sixth field, with a median of 5 fields to the first flag ... one field less corresponds to roughly 1.3 years earlier"); Discussion ("the trend test needed a median of 5 fields, several years").
- **Verification:** `descriptives.json`: D1 median first flag at field 5, 69.2% flagged by 6; D6 median 3 (76.9%); D8 median 4 (78.5%). Median interval between the first six fields is 1.29 years (IQR 1.02–1.62); median time to field 6 is 6.45 years.
- **Problem:** "Several years" should be a number. **[Reviewer calculation]** Field 5 is about 4 intervals after baseline, ≈ 5.2 years at the median interval. 31% of reference progressors were still unflagged by D1 at field 6, ≈ 6.5 years. These are eyes that, by the reference definition, lose more than 0.3 dB/yr. Separately, the "earlier" medians of D6 and D8 are first flags, not confirmed flags. Table 2 shows D6 sensitivity collapsing from 57% at k = 3 to 25% at k = 4, so many of those first flags disappear at the next field. Reporting "median 3 fields" for D6 therefore over-states its clinical earliness even before counting false alarms.
- **Fix:** Report time to first flag *and* time to confirmed (persistent) flag in years, with the censored proportion. State plainly that with 1.3-year spacing, a trend test confirms a typical progressor about 5 years after baseline. Then give the corresponding figure under the recommended six-fields-in-two-years schedule [5] (it can be simulated by re-timing the same fields, as was done for Artes in amendment A1). That would turn the Discussion's main message ("the number and spacing of fields, not the detector") into a quantified result rather than an inference.

### 4. MAJOR — The shuffle audit is not yet a clinic tool: requirements, cut-off and error rates are unspecified
- **Location:** Abstract ("clinics can check a detector on their own records with a shuffle audit"); Key Summary Points ("a shuffle audit of a clinic's own records is a cheap first check"); Discussion ("A clinic that adopts a new detector can therefore shuffle its own records, re-run the detector and compare the flag rate with the nominal level; a large excess is a warning sign").
- **Problems, each with a fix:**
  1. **Access.** The audit requires exporting raw pointwise fields and dates, and running the detector *outside the vendor device* on re-ordered series. Commercial progression software (on-device trend and event analyses) cannot be fed shuffled orders, and proprietary AI products may not accept arbitrary inputs. *Fix:* state that the audit is feasible only where the clinic (or vendor) can run the detector on exported data. Recommend that vendors provide an audit mode.
  2. **Sample size.** The validation used 869 test eyes × 10 shuffles (`fpr_shuffle_n` = 8,690). A typical clinic or a vendor pilot may have far fewer eyes with ≥ 3 fields. *Fix:* report audit precision as a function of the number of eyes (subsample 50, 100, 200 and 500 eyes) so a clinic knows how many records it needs.
  3. **Decision threshold.** "A large excess" is undefined. **[Reviewer calculation]** with an arbitrary audit cut-off of > 7.5% against the paper's own validity labels (`metrics.json`): the audit flags 7 of 8 invalid cells but misses D6 at k = 6 (audit 4.9%, true 7.6%, CI upper 11.1%). It also raises false audit alarms in 4 of 32 valid cells: D2 k = 3 (11.7% versus 2.6%), D7 k = 5 (7.9% versus 1.2%), D7 k = 6 (11.7% versus 1.9%) and D8 k = 6 (8.7% versus 3.6%). It systematically under-estimates the worst ML cell (D8 k = 3: 17.3% versus 27.7%). *Fix:* pre-specify and report a decision rule (cut-off, sensitivity and specificity of the audit for "invalid"). Say explicitly that the audit is conservative for size-threshold rules (D2, D7) and can under-read ML models.
  4. **What it can and cannot detect.** Shuffling destroys order. It cannot reveal false alarms that come from long-term fluctuation, learning effects or reliability problems, which are also absent from the Artes weekly series (see issue 5). *Fix:* add a sentence on this scope.
  5. **What to do with the result.** *Fix:* give a one-paragraph protocol box: inputs, number of shuffles, cut-off, and the action taken if it fails (do not use the flag for escalation; require confirmation; contact the vendor).

### 5. MAJOR — The true no-change arm likely under-represents real-world false alarms
- **Location:** Methods (Artes: "Thirty patients ... 12 visual fields in 12 consecutive weeks"); Limitations ("does not capture fluctuation over months or years").
- **Problem:** The validity label of every detector depends on this single arm (30 patients, one centre). Week-to-week variability excludes seasonal, inter-visit and long-term fluctuation, cataract progression, and changes in test strategy or technician. Real clinics' false-alarm rates are therefore probably higher for all detectors, and "valid" in this paper means "valid under short-term noise". Amendment A1 (borrowing UWHVF dates) fixes the time scale for rate rules but not the noise structure. UWHVF also lacks reliability indices (Methods), so neither data set shows what reliability screening does to false flags.
- **Fix:** Re-label "valid" as "valid on short-term retest data" throughout. Add a sensitivity analysis that inflates retest noise (e.g. adds between-visit variance estimated from UWHVF stable eyes) and report how many detectors stay valid. State in Limitations whether the Artes patients were experienced perimetry takers, since a learning effect in weekly testing would bias toward improvement and fewer false progression flags.

### 6. MODERATE — Cost and escalation consequences are asserted, not quantified
- **Location:** Introduction ("A false alarm can lead to unnecessary escalation, with extra drops, laser or surgery and their side effects and costs"); Discussion ("a flag from it should not by itself trigger escalation of treatment").
- **Problem:** No downstream pathway is modelled, not even a simple one. In practice a flag usually triggers a repeat field or an earlier review before escalation, so the immediate cost of a false flag is an extra visit and test, not surgery. Without this the escalation framing (the journal-fit argument in `selected_journal.md`) is rhetorical.
- **Fix:** Add a simple, transparent scenario. For example, per 1,000 stable eyes followed to six fields: number of extra confirmation visits (from issue 1), and number reaching escalation if confirmation on the next field is required (computable from the Artes windows). Keep costs as user-replaceable inputs, not invented figures. Alternatively, soften the language to "may prompt extra testing and, if not confirmed, unnecessary escalation".

### 7. MODERATE — Generalisability to other perimeters, strategies and populations
- **Location:** Abstract; Table 1; Discussion; Limitations.
- **Problems:** (a) Three of ten detectors (D6, D9, D10) are tied to the 24-2 pattern. Nothing is said about 24-2C, 10-2 or 30-2, or about SITA Standard versus SITA Faster (the testing strategy in UWHVF is not stated). Strategy affects variability and hence false alarms. (b) GRAPE patients were young (median age 41, IQR 28–53, `descriptives.json`) against 59 in UWHVF reference eyes, with a median of 4 visits over 2.3 years. That is not a typical glaucoma clinic population. (c) Test spacing of 1.3 years in UWHVF is sparser than recommended [5]. Results on earliness are specific to that schedule. (d) D8 was transferred from Humphrey to Octopus without recalibration, a deployment scenario no clinic would use. D5 and D7 were recalibrated on GRAPE by cross-fitting, and D5 then matched D1.
- **Fix:** Add a "where these results apply" paragraph: Humphrey 24-2, sparse testing, short-term noise null. Report GRAPE D8 also after local recalibration (as for D5 and D7), so the "transfer" result reflects a plausible deployment. State what the testing strategy in UWHVF is or that it is unknown.

### 8. MODERATE — The recommendation "test more often" is inferred, not shown
- **Location:** Discussion para 5 ("Our results suggest that these strategies, rather than a more complex detector applied to the same sparse fields, are where earlier detection is likely to come from").
- **Problem:** The paper evaluates detectors, not schedules. The inference is reasonable and backed by refs [32–35], but it is the paper's most actionable message for services and it has no data from this study.
- **Fix:** Either do the re-timing analysis in issue 3 or label the sentence clearly as an interpretation drawn from prior work.

### 9. MINOR — Units and phrasing for service planners
- "Per 1,000 stable eyes" is computed on Artes windows (30 patients), not on stable eyes in a clinic. Say "per 1,000 reviews of an eye with no true change".
- Report the D1 per-1,000 CI (28–63) whenever the point estimate appears, so readers see that even the reference test's false-flag burden is imprecise.
- Give the number of reference progressors not flagged by six fields (D1: 31%) next to the median fields to first flag.

## Recommendation

**Major revision.** The paper points in the right direction for services (calibrated defaults, verify before you trust, the schedule matters more than the algorithm). But its real-world translation is single-review, PPV-free and schedule-agnostic. The shuffle audit is promising, but it is a research procedure, not yet a clinic tool. Adding cumulative false flags, PPV at stated prevalences, years to confirmed detection, an audit decision rule with error rates and sample-size guidance, and honest generalisability boundaries would make this one of the more useful implementation-relevant papers in the journal.

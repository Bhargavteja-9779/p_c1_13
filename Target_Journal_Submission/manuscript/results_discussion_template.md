# Discussion

Our main finding is that, once detectors were held to the same false-alarm rate, none detected more glaucomatous visual-field progression than a plain trend test of mean sensitivity. This was true for permutation, event-based, supervised machine-learning and self-supervised detectors, from the third to the sixth test, and the study had enough eyes to exclude gains of {{qmaxhi}} percentage points or more. By contrast, a machine-learning detector thresholded in the usual way appeared up to {{qd:sens:B8:6}} points more sensitive, because it raised false alarms in up to {{qmaxfa_b8}} of series in which nothing had changed. The apparent advantage was a choice of operating point, not a better detector.

Why does the usual threshold mislead? Setting a threshold at 95% specificity in labelled stable eyes controls false alarms only in eyes like those eyes. Labelled stable eyes are selected by their long-term course; in our data they had flat or improving slopes. Eyes seen at an ordinary review, and short series in particular, are noisier. Stability limits based on stable eyes are widely used [18, 21], and labels themselves vary between studies [22]. Shuffle calibration sidesteps both problems: it uses every eye in the clinic's records, needs no labels, and keeps each eye's own noise and test dates. In our data it brought machine-learning false alarms close to the target on true no-change data that it had never seen. It is simple enough for any clinic or software vendor to apply.

These results fit with what is known about the information in a few visual fields. In routine care, the third test came a median of {{d:s2_progressors.years_to_test3.median:1}} years after the first and the sixth a median of {{d:s2_progressors.years_to_test6.median:1}} years after the first. With so few, noisy measurements, a well-calibrated linear trend appears to capture most of the available signal. Earlier work has shown that the number and timing of tests largely determine how quickly progression can be confirmed [4, 25–27]. More tests early in follow-up [28] or risk-guided scheduling [23, 34] are therefore more promising ways to gain time than more complex detection rules, although we did not test them.

The repeated-review analysis shows a trade-off that single-review studies hide. Applied at every review, the trend test flagged {{qc:sens:D1-repeated:6}} of progressors by the sixth test, but also {{qc:fa:D1-repeated:6}} of series in which glaucoma had not changed. Requiring confirmation at two consecutive reviews kept cumulative false alarms at 5% but missed {{qmissq2}} of the {{qd1det}} progressors that the unconfirmed test found by the sixth test. Neither choice is right for every patient. A clinician may accept more false alarms in an eye at high risk, and fewer in an eye with mild, stable disease. Detectors should therefore report their cumulative false-alarm rate, so that the choice can be made explicitly. Event and trend methods [11] and different event criteria [13] have been compared before; our simplified event analysis was not Guided Progression Analysis, and its low false-alarm rate needs confirmation with the commercial software.

The external results add a practical warning. A model trained in one clinic and moved to another clinic and perimeter lost specificity. Local recalibration on the new clinic's shuffled records restored specificity, but in our data the model then detected few progressors, while the plain trend test kept its performance without any recalibration. Transferring a complex detector is therefore riskier than transferring a simple one.

Within-eye permutation methods such as PoPLR are valid by design [6] but cannot flag any eye with three tests, because there are only six possible orders. Pooling the permutation reference across patients removes this floor [29–31, 33]; in Study 2, the pooled PoPLR statistic was conservative on true no-change data and matched the trend test at three to five tests. The self-supervised order model, which learns what a real time order looks like from thousands of unlabelled eyes, also matched the trend test at three tests. Neither improved on it. We had already seen excess false alarms for a pooled and detrended method during development, so the Study 1 finding about that method is a confirmation on locked test data rather than a new discovery; the Study 2 analyses were all new.

Our study has limitations. First, the reference standard was based on the long-term slope of mean deviation, which may favour the trend test. The future-only and flat-stable references, which address incorporation bias and the improving-eye problem, did not change the conclusions, but a reference based on expert review of whole series would be stronger. Second, the true no-change arm had only {{d:artes.n_patients:int}} patients, mostly with early to moderate damage, tested over about three months, with test dates borrowed from routine care. It lacks long-term fluctuation, so false alarms in eyes with advanced damage may be higher. Third, the Study 2 protocol was written after the Study 1 results were known, and neither protocol was registered externally; in Study 1, we also changed how the validity rule was applied after seeing the results (the conclusions hold under the strict rule). Fourth, UWHVF does not include diagnoses or reliability indices. Fifth, GRAPE used a different perimeter, had only {{d:grape.md_label_pos:int}} labelled progressing eyes, and its labels came from criteria applied to the whole series. Sixth, our machine-learning detectors used summary features or a small recurrent network with a modest tuning budget; larger models such as deep forecasting [19, 20] or diffusion models [24], Bayesian spatial models [9, 10, 35] and smoothing methods [36] were not tested. Shuffle calibration can be applied to any of them, and our results predict that their advantages will shrink at matched false alarms. Finally, we did not measure the clinical consequences of false alarms or delayed detection.

# Conclusions

Across two studies, three data sources and 21 detector variants, apparent gains in early detection of glaucomatous visual-field progression disappeared when false alarms were matched. A plain trend test of mean sensitivity was as sensitive as machine-learning, permutation and event-based detectors at the same false-alarm rate. Detectors should be compared, and deployed, at a stated false-alarm rate, measured on shuffled or true no-change data and reported per review and over repeated reviews. Shuffle calibration offers a simple way to do this with a clinic's own records.

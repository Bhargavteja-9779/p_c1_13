# Discussion

In this benchmark, no detector with a valid false-alarm rate found significantly more future-confirmed progression than a simple trend test of mean sensitivity. The one detector that was significantly more sensitive, a conformal test with a pooled and detrended reference, raised a false alarm in about one in five true no-change series. LightGBM, thresholded at 95% specificity, raised false alarms in up to {{p:fpr_true_null:D8:3}} of such series. These findings are consistent with our main hypothesis. They do not show that the trend test is the best possible detector: with {{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} progressing eyes, the study could detect only large gains, and event analysis and, at six tests, LightGBM had higher point estimates with acceptable false alarms.

The usual way of measuring specificity did not reveal the false-alarm problem. All detectors had high specificity against reference-stable eyes, including D6, which flagged {{p:fpr_true_null:D6:3}} of true no-change series. By our definition, reference-stable eyes had a flat or improving long-term course, and there were only {{d:ref_stable.n_eyes:int}} of them. Such eyes are easier than the stable eyes a clinic sees at an ordinary visit, so this form of specificity is optimistic. Direct measurement on true no-change data was needed to see the problem.

The machine-learning result has a plausible explanation. A threshold chosen to give 95% specificity in {{d:labelled_val.stable:int}} labelled stable validation eyes controls false alarms only in eyes that resemble those eyes. Short no-change series from another clinic, and fields from another perimeter, may not. Stability limits based on stable eyes are used in earlier work [18, 21]; our results suggest that such limits should also be checked on true no-change data. Labels also matter: the choice of progression label strongly affects how well machine-learning models appear to perform [22].

The information in a few tests is limited. In routine care in Seattle, the third test of progressing reference eyes came a median of {{d:ref_progressors.years_to_test3.median:1}} years after the first, and the sixth test a median of {{d:ref_progressors.years_to_test6.median:1}} years after the first. Even so, the trend test had flagged only about {{ttd:D1}} of reference progressors by the sixth test, while it flagged {{x:cum:D1}} of no-change series at least once over the same looks. Earlier work has shown that the number and timing of tests largely determine how quickly progression can be confirmed [4, 25–27]. More tests early in follow-up [28] or risk-guided scheduling [23, 34] may therefore gain more time than more aggressive detection rules, although we did not test this.

Repeated looks matter in practice. Event analysis, which requires change to be confirmed on two consecutive tests, flagged only {{x:cum:D7}} of no-change series at least once across four looks, compared with {{x:cum:D1}} for the trend test, while its sensitivity at five and six tests was similar. Confirmation of a suspected change on a further test is standard clinical practice, and our results support it. Event and trend methods [11] and different event criteria [13] have been compared before; our simplified event analysis was not Guided Progression Analysis, so this comparison needs confirmation with the commercial software.

Within-eye permutation methods such as PoPLR are valid by design [6]. However, they cannot flag any eye with three tests, because there are only six possible orders. Pooling permutation references across patients, using ideas from permutation and conformal inference [29–31, 33], removes this floor, and we expected this to help. It did not: the pooled test without detrending (D5) matched the trend test almost exactly, and the detrended version was not valid. We had already seen the excess false alarms of the detrended version during development, so this part of our result is a confirmation on locked test data rather than a new discovery.

The shuffle audit is a practical tool. Any clinic or developer can run it on routine records without a retest study. In our data, it identified the excess false alarms of D6 and LightGBM at three tests, but its agreement with true no-change data fell short of our pre-specified target. It overestimated false alarms for event analysis and the rate rule, perhaps because routine-care eyes are noisier than the early-glaucoma eyes in the retest study, and it underestimated them for the GRU network at three tests and D6 at six tests. We therefore see the audit as a screening check, not a replacement for true no-change data.

For care, a false progression flag can prompt extra tests or a change of treatment, with possible side effects [2, 3]. At a false-alarm rate of {{p:fpr_true_null:D6:3}}, a clinic reviewing 1,000 stable eyes would see about {{f1000:D6:3}} false flags, compared with about {{f1000:D1:3}} with the trend test. These figures come from mostly early glaucoma and from one review each; over repeated reviews the numbers are higher. Claims of earlier detection should be read together with false-alarm rates measured on data in which the disease did not change.

Our study has limitations. First, the future-confirmed reference set was small ({{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} progressors and {{d:ref_stable.n_eyes:int}} stable eyes); only gains of {{x:mdd_pp}} percentage points or more could reach significance. Second, the reference required at least eight tests, which only {{d:uw_test_ge8.n_eyes:int}} of {{d:uw_test.n_eyes_ge3:int}} test-set eyes had, and it used the whole series, including the tests the detectors saw (incorporation bias). It was based on mean deviation, which may favour D1; the secondary PoPLR reference is not independent of it. Third, stable reference eyes had flat or improving slopes, which makes specificity optimistic. Fourth, the true no-change arm had only {{d:artes.n_patients:int}} patients, mostly with early to moderate damage, tested over about three months. It lacks long-term fluctuation, and false alarms in eyes with more damage may be higher than we measured. Fifth, UWHVF does not include diagnoses or reliability indices. Sixth, GRAPE used a different perimeter and had only {{d:grape.md_label_pos:int}} eyes labelled as progressing, and its labels came from criteria applied to the whole series; for {{x:grape_3visit}} eyes these were the same tests the detectors saw. Seventh, the question of this study arose from development work in which the Artes data had already shown excess false alarms for a pooled-detrended method; the protocol was written after that and was time-stamped only in our own repository. We also changed how the validity rule was applied after seeing the results; the conclusions hold under the strict literal rule. Eighth, we did not test every published detector, such as Bayesian spatial models [9, 10, 35], smoothing methods [36], deep forecasting models [19, 20] or diffusion-based models [24], and our machine-learning detectors had a modest tuning budget and few labelled eyes. Finally, we did not measure the clinical consequences of false alarms.

# Conclusions

In this benchmark, no detector that kept false alarms near the nominal level was significantly more sensitive than a plain trend test of mean sensitivity, from the third to the sixth visual-field test, although the study could detect only large differences. The only significant gain came with a false-alarm rate of about one in five. Event analysis with confirmation gave similar sensitivity with fewer cumulative false alarms. Before a new detector is used to guide treatment escalation in glaucoma, it should report its false-alarm rate on true no-change data, or at least a shuffle audit, including over repeated looks. Larger true no-change data sets with more advanced damage are needed.

# Discussion

In this benchmark, no detector found significantly more future-confirmed progression than a simple trend test of mean sensitivity while keeping a valid false-alarm rate. This result supports our main hypothesis. The one detector that was significantly more sensitive, a conformal test with a pooled and detrended reference, raised a false alarm in about one in five true no-change series. Machine-learning models thresholded at 95% specificity raised false alarms in up to {{p:fpr_true_null:D8:3}} of such series. The gains these detectors seemed to offer were, at least in large part, false alarms.

The usual way of measuring specificity did not reveal this problem. All detectors had high specificity against reference-stable eyes, including D6, which flagged {{p:fpr_true_null:D6:3}} of true no-change series. Reference-stable eyes are selected because their long-term course is flat, and there were only {{d:ref_stable.n_eyes:int}} of them. Such a set can be easier than the stable eyes a clinic sees at an ordinary visit. True no-change data and the shuffle audit both measure the false-alarm rate more directly. In our study, both identified the same invalid detectors.

The machine-learning result has a simple explanation. A threshold chosen to give 95% specificity in labelled stable eyes controls false alarms only in eyes that resemble those eyes. Short series, and series from other clinics, may not. Thresholds chosen on labelled stable eyes are a natural choice for machine-learning detectors of progression [16–18], but our results show that they should also be checked on true no-change data. Our external results point the same way: the model transferred from UWHVF to GRAPE lost specificity, while a simple test calibrated on the external clinic's own data did not. Labels also differ between studies, and models trained on one label may not transfer [22].

The second lesson is that the information in a few tests is limited. In routine care in Seattle, the third test of reference eyes came a median of {{d:ref_progressors.years_to_test3.median:1}} years after the first, and the sixth test a median of {{d:ref_progressors.years_to_test6.median:1}} years after the first (progressing eyes). Even so, the trend test flagged only about {{ttd:D1}} of reference progressors by the sixth test. More complex statistics did not overcome this limit without paying in false alarms. This agrees with earlier work showing that the number and timing of tests largely determine how quickly progression can be confirmed [4, 25–27]. Practical ways to gain time are therefore more tests early in follow-up [28] or scheduling guided by risk [23, 34], rather than more aggressive detection rules.

Within-eye permutation methods such as PoPLR are valid by design [6]. However, they cannot flag any eye with three tests, because there are only six possible orders. Pooling permutation references across patients, using ideas from permutation and conformal inference [29–31, 33], removes this floor, and we expected this to help. It did not: the pooled test without detrending (D5) matched the trend test almost exactly, and the detrended version was not valid. Event and trend methods have been compared before [11, 13]. In our data, event analysis was valid on true no-change data and had the highest point estimate of sensitivity among valid detectors at five tests, but its difference from the trend test was small and not significant.

The shuffle audit is a practical tool. Any clinic or developer can run it on routine records without a retest study. In our data it ranked detectors much like true no-change data did and flagged D6 and D8 as invalid at three tests. Its agreement fell short of our pre-specified target, however. The audit overestimated false alarms for event analysis and for the rate rule, probably because routine-care eyes are noisier than the early-glaucoma eyes in the retest study, whose median mean sensitivity was {{d:artes.md_proxy.median:1}} dB. We therefore see the audit as a screening check, not a replacement for true no-change data.

For treatment decisions, these results matter in a direct way. A false progression flag can lead to additional drops, laser or surgery, with side effects and costs [2, 3]. At a false-alarm rate of {{p:fpr_true_null:D6:3}}, a clinic reviewing 1,000 stable eyes would see about {{f1000:D6:3}} false flags, compared with about {{f1000:D1:3}} with the trend test. Claims of earlier detection should therefore be read together with false-alarm rates measured on data in which the disease did not change.

Our study has limitations. First, the future-confirmed reference set was small ({{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} progressors and {{d:ref_stable.n_eyes:int}} stable eyes), so confidence intervals for sensitivity were wide. The study could miss real differences of about 10–20 percentage points. Second, the primary reference was based on mean deviation, which may favour detectors based on mean sensitivity, such as D1. Against a secondary reference based on PoPLR, the pattern was the same: the largest sensitivity at three tests again belonged to the invalid D6. Third, the true no-change arm had only {{d:artes.n_patients:int}} patients, mostly with early to moderate damage, tested weekly. We gave each window the test dates of routine-care eyes, but false alarms in eyes with more damage may be higher than we measured. Fourth, UWHVF does not include diagnoses or reliability indices, so some eyes may not have glaucoma and some tests may be unreliable. Fifth, GRAPE used a different perimeter and had only {{d:grape.md_label_pos:int}} eyes labelled as progressing, and its labels came from criteria applied to the full series. Sixth, the Artes data were used once during development, to check false alarms of a candidate method; this is disclosed in the Supplementary Material. Seventh, we did not test every published detector, such as Bayesian spatial models [9, 10, 35], smoothing methods [36], deep forecasting models [19, 20] or diffusion-based models [24], and our machine-learning detectors had a fixed and modest tuning budget. Finally, we did not measure the clinical consequences of false alarms.

# Conclusions

In three independent data sources, a plain trend test of mean sensitivity was as good as any detector that kept false alarms at the nominal level, from the third to the sixth visual-field test. Detectors that appeared to find progression earlier did so largely by raising false alarms. Before a new detector is used to guide treatment escalation in glaucoma, it should report its false-alarm rate on true no-change data, or at least a shuffle audit. Faster confirmation of progression will more likely come from better-timed testing than from more aggressive analysis. Prospective studies should test this in clinical care.

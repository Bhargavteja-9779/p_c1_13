We submit our Original Research article, "False Progression Flags and Treatment Escalation in Glaucoma: Benchmarking Visual Field Detectors and Calibrating Them on a Clinic's Own Data", for consideration in *Ophthalmology and Therapy*.

Treatment of glaucoma is escalated when the visual field gets worse, and many new detectors, including machine-learning models, claim to detect this earlier. Most are judged by how many eyes they flag, so an earlier flag cannot be told apart from a false alarm. We compared ten detectors on the first three to six fields using a future-confirmed reference, a true no-change arm (patients retested about weekly), and an external data set from China measured with a different perimeter.

The main finding is practical. With three fields, a machine-learning detector flagged more progressors than a simple trend test ({{s_D8_3}}% versus {{s_D1_3}}%) but raised false alarms in {{f_D8_3}}% of true no-change series, which specificity in reference-stable eyes did not reveal. In an exploratory analysis we show that resetting each detector's threshold on shuffled copies of the clinic's own visual fields ("shuffle calibration") brought false alarms close to the nominal 5% (machine learning: {{f_D8_3}}% to {{xf_D8_3}}%), and restored specificity in a clinic using a different perimeter ({{gp_D8_3}}% to {{xgp_D8_3}}%) without any labels or retest data. Calibration has a cost: once false alarms were controlled, no detector was more sensitive than the trend test, and external sensitivity fell. With few fields, every method missed most progressing eyes, so we argue that a progression flag should guide escalation only from detectors with a demonstrated false-alarm rate. We think these results are relevant to the journal's readers because they bear directly on when a progression flag should lead to escalation of therapy, and they give clinics a concrete way to check and set any detector before relying on it.

The analysis plan was written and dated before the final test data were analysed, every deviation from the protocol, including analyses added after an internal review, is listed in the Methods, and the code and result files are available on request. Only public, de-identified data were used. The manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere, and all authors have approved it.

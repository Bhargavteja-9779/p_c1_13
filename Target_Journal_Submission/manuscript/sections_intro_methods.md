# Introduction

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness. About 76 million people had glaucoma in 2020, and this number is expected to reach about 112 million by 2040 [1]. Treatment lowers intraocular pressure and slows visual loss [2, 3]. The key clinical decision is when to escalate treatment. Clinicians make this decision mainly when standard automated perimetry shows that the visual field is getting worse.

Detecting this change early is hard. A single visual field test is noisy, and the noise grows as sensitivity falls [8]. To separate true loss from noise, clinicians need a series of tests. Expert guidance suggests six tests in the first two years [4], but routine care rarely reaches this frequency. Delays in follow-up are a documented cause of avoidable sight loss [5].

Many methods aim to detect progression from fewer tests. Trend methods fit a line through the mean deviation or through each test location [6, 12]. Event methods compare each follow-up test with a baseline, using limits taken from stable patients [7, 11, 13]. Permutation methods, such as permutation of pointwise linear regression (PoPLR), compare the eye's real test order with shuffled orders of the same tests [6]. Bayesian and spatial models share information across locations or patients [9, 10, 35]. More recently, machine-learning models have been trained to classify or forecast progression [16–21], and some newer studies use deep generative models or risk-based test scheduling [23, 24].

Two weaknesses run through this literature. First, most new detectors are judged by how many eyes they flag. More flags can mean better sensitivity, or simply more false alarms. Specificity is usually estimated indirectly: by shuffling test order, by using eyes labelled stable by another criterion, or by simulation. Only a few studies have measured false alarms on true no-change data, where the same patients were tested repeatedly over a few weeks [7]. Second, labels for "progression" differ between studies, and models trained on one label may not transfer [22]. As a result, clinicians cannot easily tell whether an earlier flag from a new method is real progression or a false alarm. A false alarm can lead to unnecessary escalation, with extra drops, laser or surgery and their side effects and costs.

We built a benchmark to answer one practical question: how early can visual-field progression be detected without raising false alarms? The benchmark has a true no-change arm, a future-confirmed reference standard, and an external data set. We compared ten detectors, from simple trend tests to permutation, conformal, event-based and machine-learning methods; three of them (D5, D6, D10) were our own ideas from an earlier development phase. We asked five pre-specified questions. RQ1: which detectors keep the false-alarm rate on true no-change series near the nominal 5% with three to six fields? RQ2: does any detector flag more future-confirmed progressors than the simple trend test of mean sensitivity while keeping a valid false-alarm rate? We hypothesised that none would do so by 10 percentage points or more (H2). RQ3: does a simple "shuffle audit", which any clinic can run on its own records, estimate the true false-alarm rate? We hypothesised agreement within 3 percentage points for most detectors and a Spearman correlation of at least 0.7 (H3). RQ4: do supervised machine-learning detectors thresholded at 95% specificity in reference-stable eyes have false-alarm rates above 5% on true no-change data (H4)? RQ5: do the conclusions hold in an external data set?

# Methods

## Study design and reporting

This was a retrospective benchmark study of public, de-identified data. We followed the TRIPOD+AI reporting guidance where it applies to detection models [32]. The analysis plan, including all ten detectors, thresholds, reference standards and statistical tests, was written down and time-stamped before the final test data were analysed (`experiments/protocol.md`, available with the code). The protocol was time-stamped in the project's version-control history; it was not registered in an external registry. Two amendments were made before any final result was seen; both are reported below. Further deviations, including analyses added after an internal review of the first draft, are listed under "Deviations from the protocol". Ideas that we developed and abandoned before the protocol was written are listed in Supplementary Note S1.

## Data sources

We used three data sets (Fig. 1).

*UWHVF (development and future-reference evaluation).* The University of Washington Humphrey Visual Field data set contains {{n_uw_fields}} 24-2 tests from {{n_uw_eyes}} eyes of {{n_uw_patients}} patients seen in routine care [14]. It includes sensitivities, age and laterality, but no diagnosis or reliability indices. We split patients, not tests, into training (60%), validation (20%) and test (20%) sets using a fixed hash of the patient identifier. Both eyes of a patient were always in the same set. The test set was used once, for the final analysis.

*Artes retest data (true no-change arm).* Thirty patients with glaucoma from Halifax, Canada, had 12 Humphrey 24-2 fields at approximately weekly intervals (median span {{artes_span}} days, IQR {{artes_span_q}}; one series spanned {{artes_span_max}} days) [7]. Glaucoma is not expected to change measurably over such a short period, so we treated any "progression" flag in these series as a false alarm. Because the tests were close together, these series contain short-term test–retest variability but not the fluctuation that occurs over months or years. The data are distributed with the visualFields R package (version 1.0.7) [37].

*GRAPE (external clinic).* The GRAPE data set contains {{n_grape_records}} visual-field records from {{n_grape_eyes}} eyes of {{n_grape_pat}} patients with glaucoma followed in Hangzhou, China [15]. Fields were measured with the G1 pattern of the Octopus 900 perimeter (Haag-Streit, Köniz, Switzerland), not the Humphrey 24-2 pattern. We used locations with a valid value at every visit of an eye, so the number of locations varied between eyes (median 59; {{g_pts_below}} eyes had fewer, minimum {{g_pts_min}}). The data set authors labelled each eye with three rule-based criteria applied to the whole follow-up: a negative slope of mean deviation with p < 0.05 (MD label), and at least two or three locations with a significant negative pointwise slope (PLR2, PLR3) [15]. We used the MD label as the primary label. These labels are not clinicians' judgements. With a median of {{grape_visits}} visits per eye ({{g_n3}} eyes had exactly three), the first three or four fields make up most or all of the series on which each label was computed, so the MD label is close to the D1 test itself.

## Reference standard (UWHVF)

For eyes with at least eight tests, we defined a future-confirmed reference from the whole series. An eye was a progressor if the slope of mean total deviation over all its tests was worse than −0.3 dB per year with a one-sided p < 0.01. An eye was stable if the one-sided p was above 0.5. Other eyes were excluded from the sensitivity and specificity analysis. The reference series includes the first k fields that the detectors see and uses a mean-deviation slope, which may favour D1 and D5. We therefore also report a secondary reference based on PoPLR over the whole series (2,000 permutations), and, post hoc, a reference computed only from the fields after the first k (at least five later fields required).

## Detectors

Each detector saw only the first k tests of a series (k = 3, 4, 5 or 6) and returned "progression" or "no progression". The two blind-spot locations were removed. All detectors used a nominal false-alarm level of 5% where a level applies.

- **D1 OLS-MD:** ordinary least-squares slope of mean sensitivity against time, one-sided t-test, p < 0.05.
- **D2 MD rate:** slope of mean sensitivity of −1 dB per year or worse (a common "fast progression" rule, no p-value).
- **D3 PLR-3:** at least three locations with a slope of −1 dB per year or worse and p < 0.01.
- **D4 PoPLR:** permutation of pointwise linear regression [6], using all possible orders of the k tests.
- **D5 pooled-shuffle conformal test:** the trend statistic of D1 is compared with the same statistic computed on shuffled series of other patients in the training set, matched by number of tests and mean sensitivity (a conformal p-value [31]).
- **D6 pooled-detrended conformal regional test:** as D5, but uses the worst of four regional trends and builds the reference from shuffled residuals after removing each series' trend, an idea borrowed from residual permutation [30]. This was the most sensitive variant during development, and its false-alarm rate on the Artes data had already been measured during development (Supplementary Note S1). Its Artes result is therefore not an independent test; its UWHVF test-set and shuffle-audit results are.
- **D7 event analysis (GPA-inspired):** a location is "deteriorated" if its loss in sensitivity from the baseline (mean of tests 1 and 2) is beyond the 5th percentile of change between test pairs less than six months apart in training eyes with similar sensitivity; progression requires at least three deteriorated locations on two consecutive follow-up tests. This borrows the logic of the Guided Progression Analysis [13] but differs from the commercial version: it uses total rather than pattern-deviation sensitivity, its limits come from routine-care test pairs rather than a dedicated retest cohort, and it corresponds to the "possible" rather than the "likely" progression rule.
- **D8 machine learning, tabular:** gradient-boosted trees on 14 summary features of the first k tests (including the level, slope and range of mean sensitivity and pointwise slope summaries), trained to predict the reference label of training eyes; the configuration with the best validation area under the curve was chosen, and the threshold was set at 95% specificity on validation-set reference-stable eyes. Because the label describes future progression, these models partly predict risk, and features of severity can raise their output even when the series does not change.
- **D9 machine learning, sequence:** a gated recurrent unit network on the raw k fields and test intervals, with the same labels, threshold rule and tuning budget (12 configurations) as D8.
- **D10 self-supervised order network:** a network trained, without labels, to recognise the true time order of an eye's tests among shuffled orders; its score was used as a PoPLR-type permutation statistic [33].


## Evaluation

*E1 future reference (RQ2).* In UWHVF test-set eyes with at least eight tests, we measured sensitivity (share of reference progressors flagged) and specificity (share of reference stable eyes not flagged) using only the first k tests.

*E2 true no-change (RQ1, RQ4).* In the Artes data we took every window of k consecutive tests. Because rate-based rules depend on the time scale, each window was given the test dates of a randomly chosen UWHVF test-set eye (ten draws per window; amendment A1). The share of windows flagged is the false-alarm rate.

*E3 shuffle audit (RQ3).* In UWHVF test-set eyes, we randomly re-ordered the first k tests (ten times) and re-ran each detector. Shuffling removes any true trend, so the flag rate estimates the false-alarm rate without retest data.

*E4 external data set (RQ5).* In GRAPE, we applied the detectors to the first three or four tests of each eye and compared flags with the data set's progression labels. D5 and D7 were re-calibrated on GRAPE itself by five-fold patient-level cross-fitting, because the perimeter differs; D8 was transferred unchanged from UWHVF; the 24-2-specific detectors (D6, D9, D10) were not applied (amendment A2).

*Real-world measures.* We expressed false alarms as expected false progression flags per 1,000 stable eyes at one review (pre-specified) and, post hoc, as the share of true no-change series flagged at least once when reviewed at three, four, five and six fields. Earliness was the number of fields until the first flag among reference progressors (the protocol specified follow-up to eight fields; because detectors were run only up to six fields, eyes not flagged by six fields were counted as seven). Post hoc, we also computed the positive predictive value (share of flags that are true progression) at the GRAPE prevalence and at 10%. Because the fields in UWHVF were a median of {{ref_int}} years apart, we also give this time in years.

## Statistical analysis

Proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of patients, which accounts for two eyes per patient and repeated windows per patient; when all or none of the eyes were flagged, the bootstrap interval has no width and we report the exact Clopper–Pearson interval instead. We compared each detector with D1 on the same eyes using the exact McNemar test and corrected p-values for the nine comparisons within each number of fields with the Holm method; as a sensitivity analysis, we also corrected across all four numbers of fields. Confidence intervals for paired differences used the same patient bootstrap. A detector's false-alarm rate was classed as valid if the upper limit of its confidence interval on true no-change data did not exceed 10% and the lower limit did not exceed 5% (that is, no clear excess over 5%); because this rule was made exact only after the results were available, we also report the stricter rule (upper limit at most 5%) and give all intervals. Agreement between the shuffle audit and the true no-change false-alarm rate was summarised by the Spearman correlation across detector–field-number combinations (bootstrap confidence interval over combinations), by their absolute difference, and, post hoc, by how often the audit classified a combination correctly as valid or invalid. Stochastic detectors were run with {{n_seeds}} seeds; tables report the seed mean and the largest seed standard deviation, and paired tests use the majority flag across seeds. Analyses used Python 3.13 with NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, LightGBM and PyTorch; the full run took {{runtime_min}} minutes on four central processing unit cores. The permutation p-values included the observed order, so they are never zero [29].

## Deviations from the protocol

(1) Validity rule: the protocol said only that the upper confidence limit of the false-alarm rate would be compared with 5%. The draft Methods, written before the final results, added an upper limit of 10% and "no excess beyond sampling error"; this was implemented as "lower limit at most 5%" after the results were available. Results under the strictest reading are reported. (2) Fields to first detection were measured up to six fields, not eight. (3) After an internal review of the first draft, we changed the paired confidence intervals from eye-level to patient-level resampling as stated in the protocol, replaced zero-width bootstrap intervals with exact intervals, added Holm correction across all numbers of fields, a bootstrap interval for the Spearman correlation, and the post-hoc analyses named above (later-field reference, cumulative false alarms, positive predictive value, GRAPE results by number of visits). (4) The description of the GRAPE labels was corrected from "clinician labels" to the rule-based criteria used by the data set authors.

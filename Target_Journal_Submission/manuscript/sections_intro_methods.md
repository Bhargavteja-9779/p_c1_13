# Introduction

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness. About 76 million people had glaucoma in 2020, and this number is expected to reach about 112 million by 2040 [1]. Treatment lowers intraocular pressure and slows visual loss [2, 3]. The key clinical decision is when to escalate treatment, and clinicians make it mainly when standard automated perimetry shows that the visual field is getting worse.

Detecting this change early is hard. A single visual field test is noisy, and the noise grows as sensitivity falls [8]. Expert guidance suggests six tests in the first two years [4], but routine care rarely reaches this frequency, and delays in follow-up are a documented cause of avoidable sight loss [5]. Many methods therefore aim to detect progression from fewer tests. Trend methods fit a line through the mean deviation or through each test location [6, 12]. Event methods compare each follow-up test with a baseline, using limits taken from stable patients [7, 11, 13]. Permutation methods, such as permutation of pointwise linear regression (PoPLR), compare the eye's real test order with shuffled orders of the same tests [6]. Bayesian and spatial models share information across locations or patients [9, 10, 35], and machine-learning models have been trained to classify or forecast progression [16–21], recently with conformal error control of forecasts [24].

A new detector that flags more progressing eyes may simply flag more eyes. False alarms matter clinically, because a false progression flag can lead to unnecessary escalation, with extra drops, laser or surgery and their side effects and costs. Yet specificity is often estimated in eyes labelled stable by another criterion, and only a few studies have measured false alarms on true no-change data, in which the same patients were tested repeatedly over a few weeks [7]. Labels for "progression" also differ between studies, and models trained on one label may not transfer [22]. Finally, a clinic that wants to use a detector has no simple way to check or set its threshold on its own patients.

We therefore asked three practical questions about the decision to escalate treatment. First, how often do ten detectors, from a simple trend test to permutation, conformal, event-based and machine-learning methods, raise false progression flags on true no-change series when given three to six fields? Second, does any of them flag more eyes that later progress than the simple trend test while keeping false alarms near the nominal 5%? Our pre-specified hypothesis was that none would do so by 10 percentage points or more. Third, can a clinic make any detector safe to use by setting its threshold on shuffled copies of its own past fields ("shuffle calibration")?

# Methods

## Study design and reporting

This was a retrospective benchmark study of public, de-identified data, reported following the TRIPOD+AI guidance where it applies to detection models [32]. The analysis plan (detectors, thresholds, reference standards, statistical tests) was written and time-stamped in the project's version-control history before the final test data were analysed; it was not registered externally. Amendments A1 and A2 were made before any final result was seen. Amendment A3 (shuffle calibration, confirmation and an enlarged reference) was prompted by an internal review of the first results; it was written and time-stamped before any A3 analysis was run, but the choice of detectors to calibrate depended on the first results, so A3 is exploratory. Ideas developed and abandoned before the protocol are listed in Supplementary Note S1.

## Data sources

*UWHVF (development and future-reference evaluation).* The University of Washington Humphrey Visual Field data set contains {{n_uw_fields}} 24-2 tests from {{n_uw_eyes}} eyes of {{n_uw_patients}} patients seen in routine care [14]. It includes sensitivities, age and laterality, but no diagnosis, treatment or reliability indices. Patients, not tests, were split into training (60%), validation (20%) and test (20%) sets by a fixed hash of the patient identifier; the test set was used once for the main analysis and once for amendment A3.

*Artes retest data (true no-change arm).* Thirty patients with glaucoma from Halifax, Canada, had 12 Humphrey 24-2 fields at approximately weekly intervals (median span {{artes_span}} days, IQR {{artes_span_q}}; one series spanned {{artes_span_max}} days) [7]. Glaucoma is not expected to change measurably over this period, so we treated any "progression" flag as a false alarm. These series contain short-term test–retest variability but not fluctuation over months or years. The data are distributed with the visualFields R package (version 1.0.7) [37].

*GRAPE (external data set).* GRAPE contains {{n_grape_records}} visual-field records from {{n_grape_eyes}} eyes of {{n_grape_pat}} patients with glaucoma followed in Hangzhou, China [15], measured with the G1 pattern of the Octopus 900 perimeter (Haag-Streit, Köniz, Switzerland). We used locations with a valid value at every visit of an eye (median 59; {{g_pts_below}} eyes had fewer, minimum {{g_pts_min}}). The data set authors labelled each eye with rule-based criteria applied to the whole follow-up: a negative slope of mean deviation with p < 0.05 (MD label, our primary label) and at least two or three significantly progressing locations (PLR2, PLR3) [15]. These are not clinicians' judgements, and with a median of {{grape_visits}} visits per eye ({{g_n3}} eyes had exactly three) the MD label is close to the trend test itself.

## Reference standard (UWHVF)

For eyes with at least eight fields, an eye was a progressor if the slope of mean total deviation over all its fields was worse than −0.3 dB per year with one-sided p < 0.01, and stable if the one-sided p was above 0.5; other eyes were excluded. Because this reference uses a mean-deviation slope over a series that includes the fields the detectors see, it may favour the trend test. We therefore also report a secondary reference based on PoPLR over the whole series (2,000 permutations [29]) and, post hoc, a reference computed only from fields after the first k.

## Detectors

Each detector saw only the first k fields (k = 3, 4, 5 or 6) with the two blind-spot locations removed, and returned "progression" or "no progression" at a nominal false-alarm level of 5% where a level applies.

- **D1 trend test (OLS-MD):** ordinary least-squares slope of mean sensitivity against time, one-sided t-test, p < 0.05.
- **D2 MD-rate rule:** slope of mean sensitivity of −1 dB per year or worse (a "fast progression" rule without a p-value).
- **D3 PLR-3:** at least three locations with a slope of −1 dB per year or worse and p < 0.01.
- **D4 PoPLR:** permutation of pointwise linear regression [6], using all orders of the k fields.
- **D5 pooled conformal test:** the D1 statistic compared with the same statistic on shuffled series of training patients matched by number of fields and mean sensitivity (a conformal p-value [31]).
- **D6 detrended conformal test:** as D5, with the worst of four regional trends and a reference built from shuffled residuals after removing each series' trend, an idea borrowed from residual permutation [30]. This was the most sensitive variant during development, and its false-alarm rate on the Artes data had already been measured then (Supplementary Note S1), so its Artes result is not an independent test.
- **D7 event analysis (GPA-inspired):** a location is deteriorated if its loss from the baseline (mean of fields 1–2) is beyond the 5th percentile of change between training test pairs less than six months apart with similar sensitivity; progression requires at least three deteriorated locations on two consecutive follow-ups. Unlike the commercial Guided Progression Analysis [13], it uses total rather than pattern-deviation sensitivity and routine-care rather than dedicated retest limits.
- **D8 gradient-boosted trees:** gradient-boosted trees on 14 summary features of the first k fields (level, slope and range of mean sensitivity, pointwise slope summaries), trained to predict the reference label of training eyes, with the configuration chosen by validation area under the curve (12 configurations) and the threshold set at 95% specificity in validation reference-stable eyes.
- **D9 recurrent network:** a gated recurrent unit network on the raw fields and test intervals, with the same labels, threshold rule and tuning budget as D8.
- **D10 order network:** a network trained without labels to recognise the true order of an eye's fields, used as a PoPLR-type within-eye permutation statistic [33].

## Evaluation

*E1 future reference:* sensitivity and specificity in UWHVF test eyes with at least eight fields, using only the first k fields. *E2 true no-change:* the share of all Artes windows of k consecutive fields that were flagged; because rate-based rules depend on the time scale, each window was given the test dates of a randomly chosen UWHVF test eye (ten draws per window; amendment A1). *E3 shuffle audit:* the flag rate after randomly re-ordering the first k fields of each UWHVF test eye (ten orders). *E4 external:* sensitivity and specificity in GRAPE against the MD label with three or four fields; D5 and D7 were recalibrated on GRAPE by five-fold patient-level cross-fitting, D8 was transferred unchanged, and the 24-2-specific detectors D6, D9 and D10 were not applied (amendment A2).

*Amendment A3 (exploratory).* (a) Shuffle calibration: for the detectors whose nominal threshold was invalid or depended on the time scale (D2, D6, D8, D9), we replaced the threshold by the 95th percentile of the detector's own score on shuffled series of the validation eyes (first k fields, ten random orders per eye); models were otherwise unchanged. Shuffling removes any trend while keeping each eye's level, spread and test dates, so this threshold targets a 5% flag rate in eyes like the clinic's own that are not changing. In GRAPE, D2 and D8 were calibrated on shuffled GRAPE series by five-fold patient-level cross-fitting. (b) Confirmation: a flag with k fields counted only if the detector had also flagged with k − 1 fields. (c) Enlarged reference: because D1–D4 use no training data, we applied them to all UWHVF eyes with at least eight fields and a reference label, across all three splits. The stated hypotheses were that no calibrated detector would have a false-alarm rate clearly above 5% (lower CI limit above 5%), that none would be at least 10 points more sensitive than D1, that confirmation would bring D6 and D8 to at most 5%, and that D2 would be more sensitive than D1 with three fields in the enlarged reference.

*Real-world measures.* We report false progression flags per 1,000 stable eyes at one review and, post hoc, the share of no-change series flagged at least once when reviewed at three, four, five and six fields; the number of fields to the first flag among reference progressors (counted as seven if not flagged by six); and, post hoc, the share of flags that are true progression (positive predictive value) at 10% prevalence and at the GRAPE prevalence.

## Statistical analysis

Proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of patients; when all or none of the eyes were flagged, we report the exact Clopper–Pearson interval. Each detector was compared with D1 on the same eyes with the exact McNemar test, with Holm correction within each number of fields and, as a sensitivity analysis, across all numbers of fields; CIs for paired differences used the same patient bootstrap. Stochastic detectors were run with {{n_seeds}} seeds; tables give seed means and paired tests use the majority flag across seeds. A false-alarm rate was classed as valid if its CI upper limit was at most 10% and its lower limit at most 5% (no clear excess); because this rule was made exact after the main results were available, we also report the stricter rule (upper limit at most 5%). Agreement between the shuffle audit and the true false-alarm rate was summarised by the Spearman correlation across detector–field-number combinations (bootstrap CI), their absolute differences and, post hoc, the audit's accuracy in classifying combinations as valid or invalid. Analyses used Python 3.13 with NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, LightGBM and PyTorch; the main run took {{runtime_min}} minutes and amendment A3 {{a3_runtime}} minutes on four processor cores.

## Deviations from the protocol

(1) The validity rule was made exact after the main results were available (see above). (2) Fields to first detection were followed to six, not eight, fields. (3) After the internal review we corrected the paired CIs to patient-level resampling as the protocol stated, replaced zero-width bootstrap intervals with exact intervals, added Holm correction across numbers of fields, a CI for the Spearman correlation, and the post-hoc analyses named above. (4) The GRAPE labels were first described as clinician labels; this was corrected. (5) Amendment A3 was added after the internal review, before its analyses were run (exploratory).

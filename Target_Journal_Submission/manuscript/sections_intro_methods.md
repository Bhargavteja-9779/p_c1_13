# Introduction

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness [1]. An estimated 76 million people aged 40–80 years had glaucoma in 2020, and this number was projected to reach about 112 million by 2040 [1]. Treatment lowers intraocular pressure and slows visual loss [2, 3]. The key clinical decision is when to escalate treatment. Clinicians make this decision mainly when standard automated perimetry shows that the visual field is getting worse.

Detecting this change early is hard. A single visual field test is noisy, and in damaged areas the variability is so large that sensitivities below about 15–19 dB carry little information [8]. To separate true loss from noise, clinicians need a series of tests. Expert guidance suggests six tests in the first two years [4], but routine care rarely reaches this frequency [27]. Delays in follow-up are a documented cause of avoidable sight loss [5].

Many methods aim to detect progression from fewer tests. Trend methods fit a line through a global index, such as the mean deviation or the visual field index [11, 12], or through each test location [6]. Event methods compare each follow-up test with a baseline, using limits taken from stable patients [7, 11, 13]; Guided Progression Analysis is the most widely used example [7, 11]. Permutation methods, such as permutation of pointwise linear regression (PoPLR), compare the eye's real test order with shuffled orders of the same tests [6]. Bayesian and spatial models share information across locations or patients [9, 10, 35]. More recently, machine-learning models have been trained to classify or forecast progression [16–21], and some newer studies use deep generative models or risk-based test scheduling [23, 24].

Two weaknesses run through this literature. First, most new detectors are judged by how many eyes they flag. More flags can mean better sensitivity, or simply more false alarms. Specificity is usually estimated indirectly: by shuffling test order [6, 10], by using eyes labelled stable by another criterion [21], or by simulation [11, 25, 26]. Few studies have measured false alarms on true no-change data, where the same patients were tested repeatedly over about three months [7, 35]. It is not known whether the indirect estimates agree with the direct ones. Second, labels for "progression" differ between studies, and the choice of label strongly affects how well machine-learning models appear to perform [22]. As a result, clinicians cannot easily tell whether an earlier flag from a new method is real progression or a false alarm. A false alarm can prompt extra testing or unnecessary escalation, with extra drops, laser or surgery and their side effects [2, 3].

We addressed this problem in two stages. In Study 1, we built an open benchmark of ten detectors, from simple trend tests to permutation, conformal, event-based and machine-learning methods, with a true no-change arm, a future-confirmed reference and an external clinic. Study 1 showed that the detectors that flagged more eyes also raised more false alarms, but it had too few reference eyes to compare detectors precisely. In Study 2, we therefore held every detector to the same false-alarm rate with a simple procedure that any clinic can apply to its own records: calibrating each detector's threshold on randomly re-ordered ("shuffled") test series. We then asked whether, at matched false alarms, any detector would detect at least 10 percentage points more future-confirmed progression than the trend test of mean sensitivity. Our hypothesis was that none would.

# Methods

## Study design and reporting

This was a two-stage retrospective benchmark study of public, de-identified data. We followed the TRIPOD+AI reporting guidance where it applies to detection models [32]. Before each stage, the analysis plan (detectors, thresholds, reference standards, hypotheses and statistical tests) was written down and time-stamped by a commit in the study's version-control repository; neither plan was registered with an external registry. The Study 1 plan was written before the Study 1 test data were analysed. The Study 2 plan was written after the Study 1 results were known, but before any Study 2 analysis was run. Deviations and analyses added after internal review are labelled as such. Ideas that we developed and abandoned during the study are listed in the Supplementary Material.

## Data sources

We used three data sets (Fig. 1).

*UWHVF (development and future-reference evaluation).* The University of Washington Humphrey Visual Field data set contains {{d:uw_all.n_fields:int}} 24-2 tests from {{d:uw_all.n_eyes:int}} eyes of {{d:uw_all.n_patients:int}} patients seen in routine care [14] (counts from the released file; the data paper reports 7,248 eyes). It includes sensitivities, age and laterality, but no diagnosis or reliability indices. We split patients, not tests, into training (60%), validation (20%) and test (20%) sets using a fixed hash of the patient identifier. Both eyes of a patient were always in the same set. In Study 1, the test set ({{d:uw_test.n_patients:int}} patients, {{d:uw_test.n_eyes_ge3:int}} eyes with at least three tests) was used once, for the final analysis. Study 2 used all patients with cross-fitting (see below).

*Artes retest data (true no-change arm).* Thirty patients with glaucoma from Halifax, Canada, had 12 visual fields at about weekly intervals [7] (median span {{d:artes.span_days.median:0}} days; one series spanned {{d:artes.span_days.max:0}} days). Glaucoma does not change measurably over this period, so any "progression" flag in these series is a false alarm. The data are distributed with the visualFields R package (version 1.0.7).

*GRAPE (external clinic).* The GRAPE data set contains {{d:grape.n_records:int}} visual-field records from {{d:grape.n_eyes:int}} eyes of {{d:grape.n_patients:int}} patients with glaucoma followed in Hangzhou, China [15]. Fields were measured with a different perimeter and test grid ({{d:grape.n_points.median:int}} analysable locations per field, median), and each eye carries progression labels supplied with the data set, assigned with three criteria (mean deviation, and two pointwise linear regression rules, PLR2 and PLR3) applied to the whole follow-up. We used the mean-deviation label as the primary label and report the other two in the Supplementary Material. Of the {{d:grape.n_eyes:int}} eyes, {{d:grape.category.OAG:int}} had open-angle and {{d:grape.category.ACG:int}} angle-closure glaucoma.

## Reference standard (UWHVF, Study 1)

For eyes with at least eight tests, we defined a future-confirmed reference from the whole series. An eye was a progressor if the slope of mean total deviation over all its tests was worse than −0.3 dB per year with a one-sided p < 0.01. An eye was stable if the one-sided p was above 0.5, which means that its estimated full-series slope was zero or positive. Other eyes were excluded from the sensitivity and specificity analysis. Of {{d:uw_test_ge8.n_eyes:int}} test-set eyes with at least eight tests, {{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} were progressors, {{d:ref_stable.n_eyes:int}} were stable and {{d:ref_excluded_eyes:int}} were excluded. The reference uses the whole series, including the first k tests that the detectors see. Because this reference is based on mean deviation, we also report a secondary reference based on PoPLR over the whole series (2,000 permutations; progressor p < 0.01, stable p > 0.5). It identified {{x:sec_ref_prog}} progressors, which include all {{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} primary progressors.

## Study 1 detectors

Each detector saw only the first k tests of a series (k = 3, 4, 5 or 6) and returned "progression" or "no progression". The two blind-spot locations were removed. All detectors used a nominal false-alarm level of 5% where a level applies.

- **D1 Trend (mean sensitivity):** ordinary least-squares slope of mean sensitivity against time, one-sided t-test, p < 0.05.
- **D2 Rate rule:** slope of mean sensitivity of −1 dB per year or worse (a common "fast progression" rule, no p-value).
- **D3 PLR-3 (pointwise rule):** at least three locations with a slope of −1 dB per year or worse and p < 0.01.
- **D4 PoPLR:** permutation of pointwise linear regression [6], using all possible orders of the k tests. With three tests there are only six orders, so the smallest possible p-value is 1/6 and D4 cannot flag at the 5% level.
- **D5 Pooled conformal:** the trend statistic of D1 is compared with the same statistic computed on shuffled series of other patients in the training set, matched by number of tests and mean sensitivity (a conformal p-value [31]).
- **D6 Pooled-detrended conformal:** as D5, but uses the worst of four regional trends and builds the reference from detrended, shuffled series [30]. It was the most sensitive variant during development, where it had already shown excess false alarms on the Artes data (Supplementary Material).
- **D7 Event analysis:** a location is "deteriorated" if its loss from the baseline (mean of tests 1 and 2) is beyond the 5th percentile of short-interval test–retest change in training eyes with similar sensitivity; progression requires at least three deteriorated locations on two consecutive follow-up tests. This is a simplified event analysis on raw sensitivities, loosely modelled on Guided Progression Analysis [7, 11]; it does not use pattern deviation. D7 needs at least four tests.
- **D8 LightGBM (tabular machine learning):** gradient-boosted trees on 14 summary features of the first k tests, trained to predict the reference label in {{d:labelled_train.progressors:int}} progressing and {{d:labelled_train.stable:int}} stable training eyes. Model selection and the threshold (95% specificity) used the validation eyes ({{d:labelled_val.progressors:int}} progressing, {{d:labelled_val.stable:int}} stable), a choice similar to stability limits derived from stable eyes in earlier work [18, 21].
- **D9 GRU (sequence machine learning):** a gated recurrent unit network on the raw k fields and test intervals, with the same labels, threshold rule and tuning budget (12 configurations) as D8.
- **D10 Order network (self-supervised):** a network trained, without labels, to recognise the true time order of an eye's tests among shuffled orders; its score was used as a PoPLR-type permutation statistic [33].

Permutation p-values were computed so that they can never be zero [29]. Stochastic detectors (D4–D6, D8–D10) were run with five seeds. All detectors were fitted on training and validation data only.

## Study 1 evaluation

*E1 future reference:* sensitivity and specificity in test-set eyes with at least eight tests, using only the first k tests. *E2 true no-change:* the share of flagged windows among all windows of k consecutive Artes tests ({{d:artes.windows.3:int}} to {{d:artes.windows.6:int}} windows). Because rate-based rules depend on time, each window was given the test dates of a randomly chosen UWHVF test-set eye (ten draws per window; amendment A1). *E3 shuffle audit:* the flag rate after randomly re-ordering the first k tests of test-set eyes (ten orders per eye), as an estimate of the false-alarm rate that needs no retest data. *E4 external clinic:* agreement with GRAPE's labels at three and four tests; D5 and D7 were re-calibrated on GRAPE by patient-level cross-fitting because the perimeter differs, D8 was transferred unchanged, and the 24-2-specific detectors were not applied (amendment A2). For {{x:grape_3visit}} GRAPE eyes with only three visits, the labels were based on the same tests that the detectors saw.

## Study 2: comparison at matched false alarms

*Design.* We used 5-fold patient-level cross-fitting across all UWHVF patients. In each fold, detectors were fitted and calibrated on four fifths of the patients and evaluated on the remaining fifth, so that every eye was evaluated once, by detectors that had never seen it. Machine-learning models used a further patient-level split of the fitting patients (75% for fitting, 25% for model selection). Of {{d:s2_all.n_eyes:int}} eyes of {{d:s2_all.n_patients:int}} patients with at least eight tests, {{d:s2_progressors.n_eyes:int}} were progressors and {{d:s2_stable.n_eyes:int}} were stable by the Study 1 reference (R1). Because R1-stable eyes have flat or improving slopes, we also used a "flat stable" reference (R2: full-series slope between −0.3 and +0.3 dB per year and one-sided p > 0.1; {{d:s2_flat_stable.n_eyes:int}} eyes) and a future-only reference that ignores the tests the detector saw (R3: the R1 rule applied to tests k+1 onwards, requiring at least five such tests).

*Shuffle calibration.* For a detector that produces a score, the threshold at k tests was the 95th percentile of the score over shuffled versions of the first k tests of the calibration eyes (20 random non-identity orders per eye, each eye keeping its own test dates). Shuffling removes any time trend but keeps each eye's level, noise, damage pattern and test spacing. The threshold therefore targets a 5% false-alarm rate per review in the clinic's own population, without a retest study.

*Detectors.* Ten per-review detectors were compared. D1 (the trend test, p < 0.05) and D7 (event analysis) were as in Study 1. Six detectors were shuffle-calibrated versions of established ideas: S1, the trend t-statistic; S2, the rate of change of mean sensitivity; S3, the PoPLR statistic [6], pooled across patients so that it can flag with three tests; S4, event analysis with its change-limit percentile (1% to 20%) and number of locations (two to four) chosen as the most liberal combination with at most 5% shuffled false alarms; S5, the Study 1 gradient-boosted-tree model (LightGBM); and S7, the Study 1 GRU network. S6 was a self-supervised detector: a LightGBM model trained, without any progression labels, to tell an eye's real test order from shuffled orders of the same tests, using all calibration eyes [33]. B8 was the Study 1 LightGBM model with its usual threshold (95% specificity on labelled stable eyes). Stochastic detectors were run with three seeds.

*Repeated reviews.* In practice, a detector is applied again at every new test. We therefore also compared four detectors designed to keep the cumulative false-alarm rate over the reviews at three, four, five and six tests at 5%: Q1, a sequential trend test with one common per-review level; Q2, a confirmed trend test that requires the trend criterion to be met at two consecutive reviews; Q3, a confirmed shuffle-calibrated event analysis; and Q4, a sequential version of S6. Their levels were chosen on shuffled six-test sequences of the calibration eyes. D1 and D7 applied at every review served as comparators.

*Evaluation.* We measured sensitivity (R1 and R3) and specificity (R1 and R2) in held-out eyes using the first k tests, and false alarms on the Artes series as in Study 1. For repeated reviews, we measured the share of progressors and of true no-change series flagged at least once by the sixth test; for Artes, the first six tests of each series were used, with one set of borrowed test dates kept across reviews. In GRAPE, D1, S1, S5, S6 and B8 were applied at three and four tests, with thresholds either taken from UWHVF or recalibrated on GRAPE's own shuffled series by 5-fold patient-level cross-fitting ("local recalibration").

## Statistical analysis

Proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of patients, which accounts for two eyes per patient and repeated windows per patient. Each detector was compared with D1 on the same eyes with the exact McNemar test, using each eye's majority flag across seeds, and p-values were corrected with the Holm method within each number of tests. A detector was called valid if, on true no-change data, the lower confidence limit of its false-alarm rate was at or below 5% and the upper limit at or below 10%. This rule differs from the literal wording of the Study 1 protocol (upper limit at or below 5%), which no detector near the nominal rate could meet with 30 patients; the rule was fixed in our draft Methods before the Study 1 results were available and made operational afterwards. The full Study 1 statistical methods, this deviation and a sensitivity analysis under the literal rule are given in the Supplementary Material. False alarms are also expressed as false flags per 1,000 stable eyes at one review. Analyses used Python 3.13 with NumPy, SciPy, pandas, scikit-learn, LightGBM and PyTorch on a 4-core CPU; the Study 1 run took {{m:runtime_min}} minutes.

In Study 2, the same bootstrap and exact McNemar methods were used, with Holm correction across the eight calibrated detectors (D7 and S1–S7) within each number of tests; B8 is reported without adjustment. The pre-specified hypotheses were that every shuffle-calibrated detector would be valid on true no-change data (H6); that at least one would be at least 10 percentage points more sensitive than D1 with a significant McNemar test (H7, our main question, which we expected to be rejected); that at a cumulative false-alarm rate of about 5% the best repeated-review detector would be non-inferior to D1 applied at every review, with a margin of 10 percentage points (H8); and that local recalibration in GRAPE would restore a specificity of at least 90% (H9). The Study 2 run took {{q2:runtime_min}} minutes.

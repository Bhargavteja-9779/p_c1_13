# Introduction

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness [1]. An estimated 76 million people aged 40–80 years had glaucoma in 2020, and this number was projected to reach about 112 million by 2040 [1]. Treatment lowers intraocular pressure and slows visual loss [2, 3]. The key clinical decision is when to escalate treatment. Clinicians make this decision mainly when standard automated perimetry shows that the visual field is getting worse.

Detecting this change early is hard. A single visual field test is noisy, and in damaged areas the variability is so large that sensitivities below about 15–19 dB carry little information [8]. To separate true loss from noise, clinicians need a series of tests. Expert guidance suggests six tests in the first two years [4], but routine care rarely reaches this frequency [27]. Delays in follow-up are a documented cause of avoidable sight loss [5].

Many methods aim to detect progression from fewer tests. Trend methods fit a line through a global index, such as the mean deviation or the visual field index [11, 12], or through each test location [6]. Event methods compare each follow-up test with a baseline, using limits taken from stable patients [7, 11, 13]; Guided Progression Analysis is the most widely used example [7, 11]. Permutation methods, such as permutation of pointwise linear regression (PoPLR), compare the eye's real test order with shuffled orders of the same tests [6]. Bayesian and spatial models share information across locations or patients [9, 10, 35]. More recently, machine-learning models have been trained to classify or forecast progression [16–21], and some newer studies use deep generative models or risk-based test scheduling [23, 24].

Two weaknesses run through this literature. First, most new detectors are judged by how many eyes they flag. More flags can mean better sensitivity, or simply more false alarms. Specificity is usually estimated indirectly: by shuffling test order [6, 10], by using eyes labelled stable by another criterion [21], or by simulation [11, 25, 26]. Few studies have measured false alarms on true no-change data, where the same patients were tested repeatedly over about three months [7, 35]. It is not known whether the indirect estimates agree with the direct ones. Second, labels for "progression" differ between studies, and the choice of label strongly affects how well machine-learning models appear to perform [22]. As a result, clinicians cannot easily tell whether an earlier flag from a new method is real progression or a false alarm. A false alarm can prompt extra testing or unnecessary escalation, with extra drops, laser or surgery and their side effects [2, 3].

We built an open benchmark to answer one practical question: how early can visual-field progression be detected without raising false alarms? The benchmark has a true no-change arm, a future-confirmed reference standard, and an external clinic. We compared ten detectors, from simple trend tests to permutation, conformal, event-based and machine-learning methods. We also tested whether a simple "shuffle audit", which any clinic can run on its own records, estimates the true false-alarm rate. Our main hypothesis was that no detector would flag substantially more future-confirmed progressors than the simple trend test of mean sensitivity while keeping a valid false-alarm rate on true no-change data.

# Methods

## Study design and reporting

This was a retrospective benchmark study of public, de-identified data. We followed the TRIPOD+AI reporting guidance where it applies to detection models [32]. The analysis plan, including all ten detectors, thresholds, reference standards and statistical tests, was written down before the final test data were analysed and time-stamped by a commit in the study's version-control repository (`experiments/protocol.md`); it was not registered with an external registry. Two amendments were made before any final result was seen; both are reported below. One further change concerns the validity rule and is described under Statistical analysis. Ideas that we developed and abandoned during the study are listed in the Supplementary Material.

## Data sources

We used three data sets (Fig. 1).

*UWHVF (development and future-reference evaluation).* The University of Washington Humphrey Visual Field data set contains {{d:uw_all.n_fields:int}} 24-2 tests from {{d:uw_all.n_eyes:int}} eyes of {{d:uw_all.n_patients:int}} patients seen in routine care [14] (counts from the released file; the data paper reports 7,248 eyes). It includes sensitivities, age and laterality, but no diagnosis or reliability indices. We split patients, not tests, into training (60%), validation (20%) and test (20%) sets using a fixed hash of the patient identifier. Both eyes of a patient were always in the same set. The test set ({{d:uw_test.n_patients:int}} patients, {{d:uw_test.n_eyes_ge3:int}} eyes with at least three tests) was used once, for the final analysis.

*Artes retest data (true no-change arm).* Thirty patients with glaucoma from Halifax, Canada, had 12 visual fields at about weekly intervals [7] (median span {{d:artes.span_days.median:0}} days; one series spanned {{d:artes.span_days.max:0}} days). Glaucoma does not change measurably over this period, so any "progression" flag in these series is a false alarm. The data are distributed with the visualFields R package (version 1.0.7).

*GRAPE (external clinic).* The GRAPE data set contains {{d:grape.n_records:int}} visual-field records from {{d:grape.n_eyes:int}} eyes of {{d:grape.n_patients:int}} patients with glaucoma followed in Hangzhou, China [15]. Fields were measured with a different perimeter and test grid ({{d:grape.n_points.median:int}} analysable locations per field, median), and each eye carries progression labels supplied with the data set, assigned with three criteria (mean deviation, and two pointwise linear regression rules, PLR2 and PLR3) applied to the whole follow-up. We used the mean-deviation label as the primary label and report the other two in the Supplementary Material. Of the {{d:grape.n_eyes:int}} eyes, {{d:grape.category.OAG:int}} had open-angle and {{d:grape.category.ACG:int}} angle-closure glaucoma.

## Reference standard (UWHVF)

For eyes with at least eight tests, we defined a future-confirmed reference from the whole series. An eye was a progressor if the slope of mean total deviation over all its tests was worse than −0.3 dB per year with a one-sided p < 0.01. An eye was stable if the one-sided p was above 0.5, which means that its estimated full-series slope was zero or positive. Other eyes were excluded from the sensitivity and specificity analysis. Of {{d:uw_test_ge8.n_eyes:int}} test-set eyes with at least eight tests, {{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} were progressors, {{d:ref_stable.n_eyes:int}} were stable and {{d:ref_excluded_eyes:int}} were excluded. The reference uses the whole series, including the first k tests that the detectors see. Because this reference is based on mean deviation, we also report a secondary reference based on PoPLR over the whole series (2,000 permutations; progressor p < 0.01, stable p > 0.5). It identified {{x:sec_ref_prog}} progressors, which include all {{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} primary progressors.

## Detectors

Each detector saw only the first k tests of a series (k = 3, 4, 5 or 6) and returned "progression" or "no progression". The two blind-spot locations were removed. All detectors used a nominal false-alarm level of 5% where a level applies.

- **D1 Trend (mean sensitivity):** ordinary least-squares slope of mean sensitivity against time, one-sided t-test, p < 0.05.
- **D2 Rate rule:** slope of mean sensitivity of −1 dB per year or worse (a common "fast progression" rule, no p-value).
- **D3 PLR-3 (pointwise rule):** at least three locations with a slope of −1 dB per year or worse and p < 0.01.
- **D4 PoPLR:** permutation of pointwise linear regression [6], using all possible orders of the k tests. With three tests there are only six orders, so the smallest possible p-value is 1/6 and D4 cannot flag at the 5% level.
- **D5 Pooled conformal:** the trend statistic of D1 is compared with the same statistic computed on shuffled series of other patients in the training set, matched by number of tests and mean sensitivity (a conformal p-value [31]).
- **D6 Pooled-detrended conformal:** as D5, but uses the worst of four regional trends and builds the reference from detrended, shuffled series [30]. This variant was the most sensitive approach during development, and development checks had already shown excess false alarms on the Artes data (Supplementary Material); it was retained so that the final evaluation would confirm or refute that finding on locked test data.
- **D7 Event analysis:** a location is "deteriorated" if its loss from the baseline (mean of tests 1 and 2) is beyond the 5th percentile of short-interval test–retest change in training eyes with similar sensitivity; progression requires at least three deteriorated locations on two consecutive follow-up tests. This is a simplified event analysis on raw sensitivities, loosely modelled on Guided Progression Analysis [7, 11]; it does not use pattern deviation. D7 needs at least four tests.
- **D8 LightGBM (tabular machine learning):** gradient-boosted trees on 14 summary features of the first k tests, trained to predict the reference label in {{d:labelled_train.progressors:int}} progressing and {{d:labelled_train.stable:int}} stable training eyes. Model selection and the threshold (95% specificity) used the validation eyes ({{d:labelled_val.progressors:int}} progressing, {{d:labelled_val.stable:int}} stable), a choice similar to stability limits derived from stable eyes in earlier work [18, 21].
- **D9 GRU (sequence machine learning):** a gated recurrent unit network on the raw k fields and test intervals, with the same labels, threshold rule and tuning budget (12 configurations) as D8.
- **D10 Order network (self-supervised):** a network trained, without labels, to recognise the true time order of an eye's tests among shuffled orders; its score was used as a PoPLR-type permutation statistic [33].

Permutation p-values were computed so that they can never be zero [29]. Stochastic detectors (D4–D6, D8–D10) were run with five seeds; we report the mean over seeds and give the standard deviation across seeds in the supplementary results table. D8 and D9 had the same tuning budget (12 configurations each) and, like all other detectors, used only training and validation data.

## Evaluation

*Number of tests (k).* With routine spacing in UWHVF, the third test of reference eyes was done a median of {{d:ref_progressors.years_to_test3.median:1}} years (progressors) and {{d:ref_stable.years_to_test3.median:1}} years (stable eyes) after the first, and the sixth test a median of {{d:ref_progressors.years_to_test6.median:1}} and {{d:ref_stable.years_to_test6.median:1}} years after the first.

*E1 future reference.* In UWHVF test-set eyes with at least eight tests, we measured sensitivity (share of reference progressors flagged) and specificity (share of reference stable eyes not flagged) using only the first k tests.

*E2 true no-change.* In the Artes data we took every window of k consecutive tests. Because rate-based rules depend on the time scale, each window was given the test dates of a randomly chosen UWHVF test-set eye (ten draws per window; amendment A1). There were {{d:artes.windows.3:int}}, {{d:artes.windows.4:int}}, {{d:artes.windows.5:int}} and {{d:artes.windows.6:int}} windows for k = 3, 4, 5 and 6. The share of windows flagged is the false-alarm rate.

*E3 shuffle audit.* In UWHVF test-set eyes, we randomly re-ordered the first k tests (ten times) and re-ran each detector. Shuffling breaks the link between test order and time, so the flag rate approximates the false-alarm rate without retest data. Random orders include the original order (with probability 1/k!), so eyes with real progression can still be flagged; the audit is therefore expected to be slightly conservative.

*E4 external clinic.* In GRAPE, we applied the detectors to the first three or four tests of each eye and compared flags with the data set's progression labels. At three tests, the {{x:grape_3visit}} eyes with only three visits had labels based on the same tests the detectors saw. D5 and D7 were re-calibrated on GRAPE itself by five-fold patient-level cross-fitting, because the perimeter and its decibel scale differ; D8 was transferred unchanged from UWHVF; the 24-2-specific detectors (D6, D9, D10) were not applied (amendment A2).

## Statistical analysis

Proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of patients, which accounts for two eyes per patient and repeated windows per patient. We compared each detector with D1 on the same eyes using the exact McNemar test and corrected p-values for the nine comparisons within each number of tests and endpoint with the Holm method. For stochastic detectors, the paired tests used each eye's majority flag across seeds. Confidence intervals for paired differences came from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of eyes. Because of the majority flag, paired differences can differ slightly from differences between the seed-averaged percentages in the tables. With {{d:ref_progressors.n_eyes:int}} progressors and nine comparisons, a sensitivity gain over D1 could reach Holm-adjusted significance only if at least {{x:mdd_eyes}} more progressors were flagged ({{x:mdd_pp}} percentage points). The protocol stated that validity would be judged by comparing the upper confidence limit of the false-alarm rate on true no-change data with 5%. Read literally (upper limit at or below 5%), this rule could not be met even by D1, whose false-alarm rate was close to the nominal 5%, because the true no-change arm has only 30 patients. Before the final results were available, the analysis plan in our draft Methods specified a working rule: the upper limit must not exceed 10% and the point estimate must not exceed 5% by more than sampling error. After the results were available, we made the second part operational as "the lower confidence limit is at or below 5%". We call a detector valid under this rule, and we report the strict literal rule as a sensitivity analysis. This sequence is a deviation from the protocol. To express false alarms in clinical units, we report the expected number of false progression flags per 1,000 stable eyes at one review. We also report, among reference progressors, the share flagged at least once by the sixth test, and, as a matching measure, the share of true no-change series flagged at least once across the looks at three to six tests (window starting at the first test). These cumulative measures, the strict validity rule, a confidence interval for the shuffle-audit correlation (resampling detector–test-number cells) and the GRAPE results for the PLR labels were added after internal review and were not in the protocol. Agreement between the shuffle audit and the true no-change false-alarm rate was summarised by the Spearman correlation across detector–test-number cells and by their absolute difference. Analyses used Python 3.13 with NumPy, SciPy, pandas, scikit-learn, LightGBM and PyTorch. The full run took {{m:runtime_min}} minutes on a 4-core CPU without a graphics card.
